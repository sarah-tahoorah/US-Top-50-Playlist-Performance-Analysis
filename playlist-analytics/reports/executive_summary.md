# Executive Summary
## United States Top 50 Playlist Performance and Song Popularity Trend Analysis

**Prepared for:** Atlantic Recording Corporation — Commercial Leadership
**Period analysed:** 1 January – 29 April 2024 (120 daily Top-50 snapshots)
**Basis:** 6,000 validated chart records · 118 songs · 19 lead artists
**Note:** Figures are drawn from a synthetic sample dataset built to mirror the production schema. Methodology transfers directly to live data.

---

### Headline KPIs

| KPI | Value | What it tells us |
|---|---|---|
| Average days on chart | **49.6** | Typical title holds its placement for roughly seven weeks |
| Average rank | **25.5** | Mid-chart is the centre of gravity |
| Rank Volatility Index | **8.7** | Positions move ±9 slots around a song's own average |
| Popularity trend (7-day) | **87.2** | Flat versus the prior week (−0.3) |
| Top Artist Dominance Index | **90.5** (Dust & Ember) | One artist controls a tenth of the chart |
| Explicit content share | **48%** — up from 32% | Explicit material is gaining chart ground |

---

### What the data says

**The chart is slow-moving and full.** Only about 2.5 of 50 slots turn over each day, and
total listener engagement across the playlist is flat. Every placement gained is a
placement taken from someone else. Defending a position we already hold is cheaper than
winning a new one.

**Position is won in the first two weeks.** Almost nothing debuts inside the Top 10 —
songs enter in the 30–50 range and climb. Promotional money spent after a title has peaked
is chasing a decline that the data shows is long, shallow and hard to reverse.

**Two artists dominate, by two different methods.** Dust & Ember dominates through
breadth (12 charting titles, 749 chart-days); Saint Avery dominates through quality (206
Top-10 days, the best average rank in the set). Together they hold 20–25% of the chart
continuously. These profiles need different investment models.

**Explicit and album-context releases perform better.** Explicit tracks average five more
days on chart and a two-position better peak; album tracks outlast standalone singles by
nearly nine days. Explicit share of the chart rose from 32% to 48% during the window.

**Twenty titles are leaving money on the table.** Twenty songs reached a strong peak but
fell off quickly ("Flash Hits"). They have proven demand and an unsolved sustain problem —
the clearest re-investment opportunity in the catalogue. A further 40 titles peaked weakly
and exited fast, and should be cut off from further spend.

---

### Top five recommendations

1. **Create a placement-defence budget.** Fund sustained support for every owned title
   inside the Top 20 rather than routing all spend to new launches. Churn is only 5% a
   day — holding ground is the cheapest growth available.
2. **Front-load 70% of a campaign's spend into its first 14 days.** Chart position is won
   during the early climb; post-peak spend measurably underperforms.
3. **Run a second promotional wave on the 20 "Flash Hit" titles.** Proven peak
   performance, short run — the highest-expected-return group in the catalogue.
4. **Weight release and marketing plans toward album-context and explicit-flagged
   material,** which consistently deliver longer runs and stronger peaks in a market where
   explicit share is trending upward.
5. **Concentrate discretionary marketing on the #21–50 band and stop-loss the 40 "Marginal"
   titles.** Outcomes in the lower chart band are six times more variable — that is where
   spend can still change the result. Top-10 outcomes are largely already determined.

---

### Caveats for decision-makers

Results are observational, not experimental: attribute advantages (explicit, album type)
are associations that may partly reflect where promotional spend already goes. The
dataset carries no stream, save or skip data, which would sharpen every ROI figure above.
Effect sizes should be re-confirmed on production snapshots before budget is committed;
the KPI definitions, dashboard and pipeline are production-ready as delivered.
